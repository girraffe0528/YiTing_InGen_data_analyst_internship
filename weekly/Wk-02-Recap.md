# Week 2 Recap

This week's exploratory data analysis of the Aido Rover telemetry dataset felt more familiar than I initially expected. Although atmospheric observations and robotic telemetry describe very different systems, the basic data analysis process was similar to the approach I used when working with atmospheric pressure-level datasets at NTU.

The biggest similarity was the need to understand each variable before interpreting relationships between variables. In atmospheric data analysis, I examined variables such as temperature, pressure, and other measurements across different pressure levels, paying attention to their distributions, ranges, missing values, and unusual observations. I followed the same process with the Aido Rover telemetry by examining battery SoC, LiDAR distance, wheel torque, IMU acceleration, temperature, and GPS coordinates. In both cases, simply calculating summary statistics was not enough. Visualizing distributions and temporal behavior helped identify patterns that were difficult to see from tables alone.

Missing data was another familiar issue. Atmospheric datasets can contain gaps caused by sensor, communication, or data-logging problems. The Aido Rover dataset contained a 2% simulated GPS dropout rate. Because these missing observations were randomly injected, they were classified as MCAR. The cause was synthetic, but thinking about whether missingness was random or related to environmental or operational conditions was very similar to the questions I considered when analyzing atmospheric observations.

What was more different than I expected was the importance of operational context. Atmospheric variables are connected mainly through physical processes and spatial or vertical structure. Rover telemetry is strongly influenced by operational states such as PATROL, ALERT, CHARGING, and FAULT. For example, the four wheel torque channels showed strong correlations of approximately 0.865–0.867 because they responded to the same operating state. This type of relationship is different from interpreting correlations among atmospheric variables.

Another difference was the direct connection between anomalies and operational decisions. An unusual atmospheric observation may require quality control or scientific interpretation, while an unusual rover reading could indicate a localization problem, battery issue, or mechanical fault requiring immediate attention.

Overall, Week 2 showed me that many of the analytical skills from atmospheric science transfer naturally to Physical AI telemetry. The tools and statistical thinking are similar, but the meaning of the patterns changes. Instead of asking only what a pattern says about a physical environment, rover analytics also asks what that pattern means for system health, reliability, and real-time operation.
